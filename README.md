# business-continuity-disaster-recovery
A practical guide explaining the difference between Business Continuity (BC) and Disaster Recovery (DR), why both matter, and how businesses can prepare for disruptions.
Overview

Businesses can face many types of disruptions, including:

Cyberattacks and ransomware
Hardware or software failures
Cloud or service outages
Natural disasters
Power failures
Human error
Supply-chain disruptions

Having a plan for these situations can help organizations reduce downtime and protect critical operations.

Business Continuity vs. Disaster Recovery

Although the terms are often used together, they have different purposes.

Business Continuity (BC) focuses on keeping critical business operations running during a disruption.

Disaster Recovery (DR) focuses primarily on restoring IT systems, applications, infrastructure, and data after a disruption.

In simple terms:

Business continuity: How do we keep the business operating?

Disaster recovery: How do we restore the technology and data we need?

Why Both Matter

A business may successfully recover its servers but still struggle to operate if employees, communication channels, suppliers, or essential business processes aren't prepared.

Similarly, a company may have excellent continuity procedures but still experience prolonged downtime if its critical IT systems cannot be recovered.

That is why BC and DR are best considered complementary parts of an organization's resilience strategy.

RTO and RPO
Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

RTO defines the target time for restoring a system or service after a disruption.

Example:

A critical application should be restored within 4 hours.

Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

RPO defines how much data loss, measured in time, the organization can tolerate.

Example:

The organization should be able to recover data from within the previous 30 minutes.

RTO and RPO can help organizations determine appropriate recovery and backup strategies.

Building a Basic BC and DR Plan

A practical approach can start with these steps:

Identify critical business processes.
Identify the systems, data, people, and vendors those processes depend on.
Determine acceptable downtime and data loss.
Establish recovery priorities.
Maintain reliable and tested backups.
Define responsibilities during an incident.
Document communication procedures.
Test the plans regularly.
Update the plans as the business changes.
Testing Matters

A recovery plan should not simply exist as a document.

Organizations should periodically test their procedures to discover problems before an actual incident occurs.

Testing may include:

Tabletop exercises
Backup restoration tests
System recovery exercises
Communication drills
Disaster simulations

The results of these tests can be used to improve the plans.

Important Takeaway

Business continuity and disaster recovery address different aspects of organizational resilience.

BC helps the business continue operating during disruption, while DR helps restore the technology and data required for normal operations.

For organizations that rely heavily on technology, considering both areas can provide a more complete approach to preparing for unexpected events.
